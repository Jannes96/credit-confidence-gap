The point is not that any one of these is wrong. Each is defensible on its own terms. The point is what they amount to together: a decision taken at a line nobody measured, justified by a confidence that is slightly but systematically overstated, and explained by a reason that another sample of customers would have stated differently.

The expected danger from an explanation is that it might be false — but a false explanation eventually contradicts what people observe and gets caught. What this page shows is the harder case: a correct explanation of an arbitrary model. It earns trust in a mechanism while concealing how much of that mechanism is an accident of the sample. That is the condition this project set out to characterise, and the reason every assertion here is printed beside its own evidence instead of alone.

And one more thing this case makes visible. Among the characteristics driving the score is foreign worker — an attribute whose use in a live scorecard would be unlawful under current EU and German law. It is kept in the model deliberately: removing it would hide the issue rather than resolve it, and a screen like this one is where it becomes impossible to overlook.

This is not a credit scoring system. The underlying data are 1 000 loans issued in South Germany between 1973 and 1975. They contain only applicants who actually received credit, so the model knows nothing about those refused, and defaults are deliberately over-represented (300 of 1 000) by the sampling design. The probability above is calibrated against that sample, not against any lending population, and is not a default rate. No decision about a real person should be derived from it.
